Software released before and after 8 December 2026: the PLD dates you should be able to prove

The revised Product Liability Directive covers software, but only for products placed on the market or put into service after a cut-off date. Older products stay under the 1985 Directive. If you ship software continuously, that raises dull but important engineering questions. When did each version first become available? Which updates were only patches, and which changed the product? Until when could you still supply security updates? This page lists what the text says about those dates, what it leaves open, and a checklist for keeping the dates provable. It covers dates only. For the wider evidence checklist, see PLD evidence checklist for engineers.

Short version

  • The new Directive applies to products placed on the market or put into service after 8 December 2026, i.e. from 9 December 2026 (Art. 2(1), as corrected). Products placed on the market or put into service before 9 December 2026 stay under Directive 85/374/EEC (Art. 21).
  • A substantial modification, including one made by a software update, makes the product count as made available or put into service again at the time of the modification (Art. 4(18), recital 40). The 10-year expiry period then starts again (Art. 17(1)(b), recital 58).
  • Ordinary updates that are not substantial modifications do not change the expiry period of the original product (recital 58).
  • Where the manufacturer retains the ability to supply software updates or upgrades, the product remains within the manufacturer’s control (Art. 4(5)(b), recital 19). Some defects that arise during that time, including a lack of safety-necessary updates, do not get the “defect came later” defence (Art. 11(2)).
  • The text does not say how each version of continuously updated software maps onto “the product”. Keep dated records that can answer the question either way.

The dates in the Directive (verified articles)

All references are to Directive (EU) 2024/2853 (EUR-Lex), checked against the Official Journal text and the corrigendum of 7 May 2026.

  • Art. 2(1), as corrected. The Directive applies to products placed on the market or put into service after 8 December 2026, i.e. from 9 December 2026. The original text said “after 9 December 2026”. The corrigendum published 7 May 2026 changed it to 8 December.
  • Art. 2(2). The Directive does not apply to free and open-source software that is developed or supplied outside the course of a commercial activity.
  • Art. 21. Directive 85/374/EEC is repealed with effect from 9 December 2026, but continues to apply to products placed on the market or put into service before that date.
  • Art. 22(1). Member States must bring the national measures into force by 9 December 2026. Claims are brought under national law, so check the transposing law in your Member State.
  • Art. 4(8) and 4(9). “Placing on the market” is the first making available of a product on the Union market. “Putting into service” is the first use in the Union in the course of a commercial activity, where the product was not placed on the market before. Under Art. 4(7), making available can be paid or free of charge.
  • Art. 4(18). A “substantial modification” is a modification of a product after it has been placed on the market or put into service that is considered substantial under the relevant Union or national product safety rules. Where those rules set no threshold, it is a modification that changes the product’s original performance, purpose or type without that change having been foreseen in the manufacturer’s initial risk assessment, and changes the nature of the hazard, creates a new hazard or increases the level of risk.
  • Recital 40. Where a substantial modification is made through a software update or upgrade, the modified product should be considered made available or put into service at the time the modification is actually made.
  • Art. 8(2). A person who substantially modifies a product outside the manufacturer’s control and thereafter makes it available on the market or puts it into service is treated as its manufacturer.
  • Art. 17(1). The right to compensation expires 10 years after the product was placed on the market or put into service, unless proceedings have started. For a substantially modified product, the 10 years run from when it was made available or put into service after the modification. Art. 17(2) extends this to 25 years where the latency of a personal injury prevented proceedings within 10 years, unless proceedings have started in the meantime. Recital 58 says updates or upgrades that are not substantial modifications should not affect the expiry period of the original product.
  • Art. 4(5)(b) and recital 19. “Manufacturer's control” includes the manufacturer having the ability to supply software updates or upgrades, itself or through a third party.
  • Art. 7(2)(e). When assessing defectiveness, the moment the product was placed on the market or put into service is taken into account. Where the manufacturer keeps control after that, the moment the product left its control is taken into account.
  • Art. 11(2). The exemption in Art. 11(1)(c), for defectiveness that probably did not exist when the product was placed on the market or put into service, or that came into being after that moment, does not apply where the defect is due to a related service, software (including updates or upgrades), a lack of software updates or upgrades necessary to maintain safety, or a substantial modification, provided that it is within the manufacturer’s control. Recital 51 adds that the Directive “does not impose any obligation to provide updates or upgrades for a product”.
  • Art. 9(1) and 9(3). At the request of a claimant who has presented facts and evidence sufficient to support the plausibility of the claim, the defendant is required to disclose relevant evidence at its disposal. Disclosure is limited to what is necessary and proportionate. If the defendant fails to disclose relevant evidence under Art. 9(1), defectiveness is presumed (Art. 10(2)(a)), and the defendant can rebut that presumption (Art. 10(5)). Recital 42 says this evidence can include documents the defendant must create by compiling or classifying the evidence it has.

Related, and separate from the PLD: the Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) sets obligations on support periods and vulnerability handling. It applies from 11 December 2027. Article 14 reporting obligations apply from 11 September 2026, and Chapter IV (notification of conformity-assessment bodies) from 11 June 2026 (Art. 71(2)). See SUPPORT.md and support periods.

What the text leaves open

The Directive talks about “the product” and about substantial modifications. It does not say whether each release of continuously delivered software (a new app version, a firmware build, a SaaS deploy) is a separate product placed on the market, or an update to one product. It also does not say exactly when software delivered over the air, through an app store or as a service is “first made available”. National courts and the transposing laws will settle this, and lawyers already read it in different ways. This page does not take a side. The engineering answer is to keep records that let you, or your lawyer, answer whichever question gets asked: the date of any version, and what changed in it.

Questions you may need to answer with dates

  1. When was version X first made available to users in the EU, and through which channel?
  2. Which versions were first made available on or before 8 December 2026, and which after?
  3. For each major release, did anyone assess whether it was a substantial modification? Who decided, when, and on what basis?
  4. Until when could you supply updates for a given version (your support period and end-of-life)?
  5. Which security fixes shipped in which releases, and on what dates?

Checklist: keep the dates provable

Before 9 December 2026

  1. Take a dated snapshot of your release history now. Record every version you have shipped up to 8 December 2026, with its release date, in a form that is itself dated (for example a git commit). This is the cut-off most likely to matter for the next ten years.
  2. Publish or backfill a GitHub Release (or your system's equivalent) for every version you shipped, including backports and patch releases. A tag with no release has no clear publication date.
  3. Write down which release lines are still supported and their planned end-of-support dates, before the cut-off (SUPPORT.md).

For every release

  1. Record the date of first availability per channel, not only the date the tag was created: app store approval and rollout, download page, OTA rollout start, or production deploy for hosted software. These can differ by days or weeks.
  2. Keep deploy and rollout logs for hosted and OTA-delivered software, so “first made available” can be shown, not reconstructed.
  3. Note a substantial-modification assessment for major releases: what changed in performance, purpose or type, whether the risk assessment foresaw it, who assessed it, and when. Keep the assessment itself. Whether something is “substantial” is a legal question, so record the facts and the decision rather than a conclusion you cannot back.
  4. Link security fixes to releases: a published advisory with patched versions for each fixed vulnerability, the CVE or GHSA ID in the fix commit, and a record of which release contained each fix (how mapping works, backports).

Over the long run

  1. Plan retention around the Art. 17 periods: 10 years from placing on the market (and from each substantial modification), and 25 years for latent personal injury. Agree the actual retention schedule with your counsel, taking GDPR storage limits into account.
  2. Do not delete old releases or rewrite history that your records point to. Archive build artifacts outside CI retention windows.
  3. Record when you stopped being able to update a version (end-of-life, discontinued update server, transferred product line). That date is the end of the period in which Art. 4(5)(b) control rests on your ability to supply updates.
  4. Have a named person review the record and sign off with a date. An automated list is a starting point, not evidence that someone checked it.

Where fixdossier fits (and where it does not)

The free fixdossier GitHub Action (MIT, current release v0.1.2, use itulo/fixdossier@v0) reads your GitHub Releases and tags, published security advisories, merged Dependabot and Renovate PRs, and security-looking commits. It writes a dated SECURITY-RECORD.md, security-record.json, and a SUPPORT.md with a “supported until” date per release. In commit mode, each state of the record is a dated git commit, so running it once before 9 December 2026 gives you a dated snapshot of your release and fix history as GitHub shows it:

yaml
name: security-record
on:
  workflow_dispatch:        # run once now for a pre-cut-off snapshot
  release:
    types: [published]
  schedule:
    - cron: '0 6 * * 1'
permissions:
  contents: write
  pull-requests: read
jobs:
  record:
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
        with:
          ref: ${{ github.event.repository.default_branch }}   # needed for mode: commit on release-triggered runs
      - uses: itulo/fixdossier@v0
        with:
          support_period_months: 24
          eol_date: '2030-12-31'
          contact: security@example.com
          mode: commit

It does not know your app store, OTA or deploy dates. It does not decide what counts as a substantial modification. It is not a complete inventory either: silent fixes and releases outside GitHub are missed (coverage limits). Items 4 to 6 above stay with you.
